# The p999 Ladder: Deep Latency Tails of Deployable Transport Classes Under Identical Kernel Impairment, and Why FEC-Based Recovery Sits a Category Below Timer-Driven ARQ on a Bursty-Loss Profile

*Draft — arXiv cs.NI shape. Every number traces to `docs/BENCH-netem.md`; where a number could not be located it is marked [VERIFY]. Citations to be filled are marked [CITE: ...].*

## Abstract

We measure the deep latency tail (p999) of message delivery over a bursty-lossy emulated cellular link for one representative of each deployable reliable-transport class: TLS 1.3 (JDK) over kernel TCP (CUBIC + SACK + RACK), kernel SCTP in unordered mode, QUIC (both an independent Java implementation, kwik, and Microsoft's production msquic under CUBIC and BBR), and Tessera, a research transport whose loss recovery is sliding-window random linear network coding (RLNC/FEC) with residual time-based ARQ. All arms run the same 1200 B echo workload at 50 msg/s under the same `tc netem` qdisc (LTE profile: 90 ms RTT, Gilbert–Elliott loss averaging ~4.8 % in ~5-packet bursts, 30 Mbit/s). At run lengths long enough to sample rare events (n = 20,000 messages per run for the open-loop arms; 300 s closed-loop for msquic), the p999 separates by *mechanism class* into categories — hundreds of milliseconds versus seconds to tens of seconds, a one-to-two-order-of-magnitude spread whose smallest measured gap, against ARQ's best single run, is ~9.6× (~9.6–21× across msquic's completing runs, ~18× against the middle of its range): FEC-based recovery at ~0.31 s; production ARQ (msquic, either congestion controller) at ~3–6.6 s; kernel ARQ (unordered SCTP ~16.7 s, TLS/TCP ~21.7 s). Two alibis are constrained, neither to the standard of elimination: under both CUBIC and BBR, msquic's p999 remains seconds-scale (3.0–6.6 s and 4.3–6.4 s across 3 completing runs each), so the congestion controller cannot account for the category, though at 3 runs per arm no finer comparison between the two CCs is supportable; and unordered SCTP shows a comparable spiral without any head-of-line blocking (suggesting stream semantics are not required for it, though that arm carries an unexplained anomaly we flag). The explanation *consistent with* these constraints — inferred, not packet-traced — is the lost retransmit: when the retransmission of a lost packet dies in the same loss burst, timer exponential backoff is the only exit, and timers cost seconds; FEC never needs a specific packet to survive. The measured claim is the ladder and the constraints; the mechanism account is the inference they leave standing, and Section 6 says so. The claim is scoped: one bursty-loss profile, one rate — at a 5× lower rate the FEC arm's own recorded p99 reaches 2.7 s (an in-process simulator measurement; Section 4), a boundary we report. We additionally report two methodology findings that we believe generalize: (1) short runs systematically flatter ARQ transports — the same TLS arm measured p99 ≈ 0.61 s at n = 1,500 and ≈ 19 s at n = 20,000, because the effective sample size for a tail claim is the number of *runs*, not messages; (2) UDP GSO batching silently invalidates netem benchmarks — a qdisc drops a ~50-packet superpacket as one loss event, which for msquic produced a 3.4× p999 inflation in a 30 s A/B and, before the fix, entire 300 s runs completing zero requests.

## 1 Introduction

Reliable transports are compared, overwhelmingly, at the median — and at the median the well-provisioned transports compared here are nearly identical. On our impaired link, JDK TLS over kernel TCP delivers a 1200 B request/response at a median of 120 ms, msquic at ~115 ms, and the FEC-based transport at 123 ms — within a few milliseconds of *each other*, 12–20 ms above the 103 ms raw-UDP floor. The differences that matter to interactive and real-time-adjacent applications live in the tail, and the tail is where measurement is hardest: the events that decide p999 are rare, heavy, and correlated, so both under-sampling and mechanism attribution errors are easy to make. This project's own measurement log contains several retracted tail claims, each made from a sample sized for a mean — including one mechanism claim about the FEC transport's own tail, made without measurement and later falsified by counting.

This paper makes one empirical claim, scoped, and defends it against the obvious alternative explanations:

**Claim.** On this bursty-loss profile (Gilbert–Elliott ~4.8 % in ~5-packet bursts, ~90 ms RTT), at this rate (50 msg/s) and message size, loss recovery driven by retransmission timers exhibits a p999 measured in seconds, and recovery by forward erasure coding measures ~0.31 s. The gap at p999 is a category (hundreds of milliseconds versus seconds to tens of seconds; ~9.6–21× against the best ARQ arm, ~54–70× against the kernel arms), not a percentage, and it survives giving the ARQ side its best case: a production implementation, its own benchmark tool, either of two congestion controllers, and message semantics with no head-of-line blocking. We do not claim the gap is scale-free: the timer floor depends on RTO minimums, RTT, and backoff depth, and the FEC side's tail is rate- and burst-dependent — the same FEC transport's recorded p99 at 10 msg/s on the same profile parameters is 2.7 s, measured in-process (Section 6, threat 12).

**Proposed mechanism (inferred, not traced).** At ~4.8 % bursty loss, a retransmitted packet occasionally dies in the same burst as the original. ARQ then has no signal but a timer, and retransmission-timeout (RTO) exponential backoff turns the second consecutive loss of the *same* data into a multi-second stall — in TCP's case additionally stalling everything queued behind it. FEC-based recovery is indifferent to *which* packets survive: any sufficient set of coded symbols reconstructs the window, so there is no distinguished packet whose repeated loss must be waited out. We did not capture packet traces or kernel retransmit/backoff counters during these runs; this account is the arithmetic explanation consistent with the constraints established in Section 4, not a measured one, and Section 6 carries it as a threat.

The measurement platform is deliberately mundane — one Linux box per experiment, kernel `tc netem`, loopback — because the point is a controlled comparison of recovery mechanisms, not a characterization of any real radio. (The same project's live 5G-hotspot sessions, recorded in the same log, show why: the radio's non-stationarity exceeds most mechanism effects at feasible run counts.)

Contributions:

1. A p999 ladder across four deployable transport classes plus raw UDP under identical kernel impairment, at run lengths that actually sample the tail (Section 3).
2. Constraint of the two standard alibis — congestion control (BBR vs CUBIC in msquic: both seconds-scale at p999, so the CC cannot account for the category) and head-of-line blocking (unordered SCTP: comparable spiral, with a flagged anomaly) — leaving timer-driven ARQ as the surviving explanation (Section 4).
3. Two reusable methodology results: run-length sensitivity of ARQ tails (TLS p99 moved 30× with n), and the GSO/qdisc interaction as a validity threat to any netem benchmark of a batching sender, with before/after numbers (Section 5).
4. An unusually complete threats-to-validity section (Section 6), because the FEC-side transport is a research prototype built by the authors and the comparison is on its favoured workload.

## 2 Method

### 2.1 Harness

All headline numbers come from `bench vs` (and its n = 20,000 "p999 campaign" mode): four-plus transports, one machine, one loopback interface, one `tc netem` root qdisc, identical workload. Each arm echoes 1200 B messages at 50 msg/s, round-trip latency recorded per message, with handshakes completed before the measured window. In the `vs` campaigns (the Java arms), arms are interleaved within each repetition so that host drift lands on all arms alike; the msquic measurements are a separate later pass with no interleaved control arm (Section 6, threat 6). The arms are implementations, not protocol ideals:

- **raw UDP** — the substrate; loss shows up as loss. Calibration control.
- **TLS 1.3 / TCP** — JDK TLS atop the kernel's TCP (CUBIC + SACK + RACK).
- **SCTP** — the kernel's message-oriented L4, run **unordered**: per-message reliability, no cross-message ordering — its most Tessera-like mode, chosen to remove the head-of-line variable. This arm landed on the campaign's final day; the measurement log records no provisioning, tuning, or control-run work for it. Section 6, threat 3 carries the consequences.
- **QUIC** — two implementations: kwik (independent, spec-derived Java; NewReno-class CC) and **msquic** (Microsoft's production QUIC, built from source, driven by its own `secnetperf` tool in 1200 B/1200 B request/response mode, ~50 req/s at this RTT via 5 concurrent streams), under **both CUBIC and BBR**.
- **Tessera** — the research transport: systematic sliding-window RLNC over GF(256) with adaptive redundancy, residual RACK-style time-based ARQ, receiver-driven credit congestion control (Homa-style) with a CUBIC fallback, Noise-IK/ML-KEM handshake. Unaudited prototype, v0 wire format.

The multi-box experiments run the same interleaved design detached on five cloud nodes in five regions (`ewr del blr sgp icn`), six repetitions per node.

**Percentiles and censoring.** All percentiles are computed over *delivered* messages only. Several ARQ runs did not deliver everything (TLS 15/20 runs fully delivered, worst 13,753/20,000; SCTP 1/13, worst 1,564/20,000); the undelivered messages are effectively infinite-latency observations that the percentiles omit, so the reported ARQ tails are *lower bounds*. The direction is conservative for this paper's conclusion, and delivery completeness is reported alongside every table for exactly this reason.

### 2.2 The impairment

The deep-tail campaign uses one profile, `lte`, from `bench/netem/profiles.sh`, applied as the root qdisc on `lo`:

```
tc qdisc add dev lo root netem delay 45ms 15ms distribution normal \
    loss gemodel 1% 20% rate 30mbit
```

That is 45 ms one-way delay with ±15 ms normal jitter (so ~90 ms nominal RTT on loopback, where one qdisc sits on the egress of *both* directions), Gilbert–Elliott loss with p = 1 %, r = 20 % — an **average** loss of p/(p+r) ≈ 4.8 % in mean bursts of 1/r = 5 packets — and a 30 Mbit/s rate cap shared by data and acks. The first `vs` table (Section 3.1) additionally uses `wifi-busy` (8 ms ± 20 ms pareto jitter, 3 % random loss, 5 % reorder, 80 Mbit) and `transcont` (90 ms ± 2 ms, 0.1 % loss, 1 Gbit).

Two loopback caveats attach to every number: the effective RTT is ~2× the `delay` parameter, and an *echo* workload sends both directions through the same qdisc — each message crosses the impairment twice and the rate cap is shared.

### 2.3 Closed vs open loop

The Java arms (udp, tls, sctp, kwik, tessera) are **open-loop**: the sender paces at 50 msg/s regardless of completions, so a stalled transport accumulates backlog and the stall is charged to latency. msquic is driven by its own tool in **closed-loop** request/response (5 concurrent streams), which throttles offered load during a stall. Closed-loop measurement flatters a stalling transport — its worst seconds carry fewer in-flight messages — so this asymmetry favours msquic, and the FEC-vs-production-ARQ gap survives it. It is nonetheless a real difference and is carried in Section 6.

### 2.4 Run length, and why it is the method

The campaign's defining choice is n = 20,000 messages per run (~6.7 minutes at 50 msg/s), against the n = 1,500 (~30 s) used in the first-pass comparisons. A run's p999 then rests on ~20 observations instead of ~2. Section 5.1 shows this is not a refinement but a verdict-changer. Repetition structure: Tessera 24 runs, TLS 20, SCTP 13, kwik 5, msquic 3 completing reps per congestion controller. One additional msquic rep per CC failed with a mid-run connection loss (status 111); those runs are **excluded from the reported percentiles** and noted as evidence of further tail mass beyond what the numbers show. The ragged counts are themselves data: the ARQ arms' own stalls kept blowing the per-invocation timeout guard, and when a guard fires it is the *last* arms in invocation order that vanish — the first pass starved the arms scheduled after kwik's collapsed-CC runs, and the completion pass reordered arms by importance.

Statistics are stated in paired or per-run form wherever possible (sign tests over interleaved pairs; per-run p999 medians and ranges across runs), never by pooling messages across runs: messages within a run are dependent — one loss burst poisons its neighbours — so the effective n for a tail claim is the number of runs. By that standard the msquic arms, at 3 completing runs per CC, support only range-level statements; we report them as ranges and draw no conclusion finer than the ranges carry.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 First contact, three profiles (n = 1,500 per run)

The initial `bench vs` table (2 runs/arm, one box) establishes shape, not magnitude:

| lte (~4.8 % GE loss) | delivered | p50 | p99 | p999 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| raw UDP | 90–94 % | 104–110 ms | 143–150 ms | 153–161 ms |
| TLS 1.3 / TCP | 100 % | 118–124 ms | 487–614 ms | 598–700 ms |
| QUIC (kwik) | 100 % | 9553 ms | 28,911 ms | 33,307 ms |
| Tessera | 100 % | 124–129 ms | 208–339 ms | 241–522 ms |

On `wifi-busy` (3 % random loss + reorder) Tessera's p99 was 153–169 ms against TLS's 156–309 ms and kwik's 372 ms; on `transcont` (0.1 % loss, 180 ms RTT) even the 1-in-1000 loss doubles TLS's p99 (287–351 ms vs Tessera's 192–194 ms). The kwik lte row is disqualified on its face: a loss-based NewReno at 5 % random loss collapses its window and queues (9.5 s p50 *with 100 % delivery* is a transport patiently queueing behind itself) — it measures the CC, not QUIC-the-design.

### 3.2 Powered and multi-box: the TLS comparison

Eleven complete interleaved pairs on one box (one TLS run of twelve hung outright and was killed at a 300 s guard; another delivered 1113/1500): **Tessera's p99 beat TLS's in 11 of 11 pairs** (p999 likewise), one-sided sign test p = 4.9e-4; per-pair ratio median 2.83×, minimum 1.87×. TLS's cross-run p99 spread was 49.5× (451.5 ms–22,361 ms) — not noise masking the effect but the effect itself, at run granularity.

Five boxes, 30 interleaved pairs per profile:

- **lte:** Tessera vs TLS **30/30 pairs**, median 2.96×, minimum 1.45×, sign test p = 9.3e-10, and 6/6 on every node. Full delivery: Tessera 30/30 runs, TLS 26/30 (one run delivered 35/1500), kwik 23/30.
- **wifi-busy (the honest, narrower result):** Tessera vs TLS 25/30, median 1.25×, minimum 0.93× — TLS won 5 pairs, three on one node. Under light *random* loss, kernel CUBIC+RACK is at its best and the edge is a quarter, not a triple. Tessera vs kwik here is fair (kwik's CC is not collapsed): 30/30, median 2.07×.

### 3.3 The p999 ladder (n = 20,000, lte, five nodes)

The final table, both passes combined; every run's p999 rests on ~20 observations. Percentiles are over delivered messages (Section 2.1), so the incomplete arms' tails are lower bounds:

| arm | runs | full delivery | p50 med | p99 med | p999 med (range) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| raw UDP | 20 | 0/20 (~5 % loss) | 103 ms | 145 ms | 159 ms |
| TLS 1.3 / TCP | 20 | 15/20 (worst 13,753/20,000) | 120 ms | 19,222 ms | 21,677 ms (4.9 s – 900 s) |
| QUIC (kwik) | 5 | 3/5 | 7,780 ms | 40,308 ms | 52,374 ms |
| SCTP (unordered) | 13 | **1/13** (worst 1,564/20,000) | 291 ms | 10,945 ms | 16,696 ms |
| Tessera | 24 | **24/24** | 123 ms | **209 ms** | **311 ms** (277–354) |

Note SCTP's median: 291 ms, ~3× the link floor, where every other completing arm's median is 115–123 ms. That anomaly is unexplained (Section 6, threat 3) and qualifies what the SCTP row can carry.

And msquic, same qdisc, GSO artifact removed (Section 5.2), 300 s runs, 3 completing reps per CC (the one failed rep per CC is excluded from these percentiles and noted as further tail mass):

| msquic rep set | p50 | p99 | p999 |
|---|---|---|---|
| CUBIC ×3 | 115–116 ms | 0.44–1.5 s | 3.0–6.6 s |
| BBR ×3 | 115 ms | 0.80–2.1 s | 4.3–6.4 s |

The ladder, in one column. A statistics caveat attaches to it: the Java rows are per-run medians across 13–24 runs, while the msquic rows are min–max ranges across 3 runs each — the source log records only the ranges, not per-rep values, so a comparable per-CC median cannot be computed from it. The ordering is robust to that (and to the differing run lengths); the exact ratios are not:

| transport class | p999 | statistic |
|---|---|---|
| FEC + residual ARQ (Tessera) | **0.31 s** | median of 24 runs |
| production ARQ (msquic, cubic or bbr) | ~3–6.6 s | range of 3 runs per CC |
| kernel ARQ, no HOL (SCTP unordered) | ~16.7 s | median of 13 runs |
| kernel ARQ + HOL (TLS/TCP) | ~21.7 s | median of 20 runs |

The smallest gap in the ladder — Tessera to msquic — is ~9.6× against msquic's best single run and ~21× against its worst; the source log's own summary, scaled to the middle of msquic's range, states the remaining gap as ~18×. Note that using the range's low end as the denominator compares Tessera's *median* to msquic's *best of three*, which understates the typical gap. The kernel arms sit ~54–70× above the FEC row (SCTP 16,696/311 ≈ 54×; TLS 21,677/311 ≈ 70×). The class boundary is the result; the exact ratios carry the caveats of Sections 2.3 and 6.

**Delivery completeness is part of the result.** Tessera delivered 480,000/480,000 messages across the campaign — the only reliable arm that never dropped one. SCTP unordered completed full delivery in 1 of 13 runs (one run delivered 8 % of its messages); TLS in 15 of 20. Tessera's cross-run p999 range (277–354 ms, five machines) is tighter than TLS's p999 varied *within* a single run.

## 4 Why: the alibis constrained, and the explanation left standing

**What is measured and what is inferred.** The measured facts are the ladder and the two constraints below. The causal account — the lost retransmit entering RTO exponential backoff — is the arithmetic explanation *consistent with* those facts; we did not instrument it (no packet captures, no `ss -ti` retransmit/backoff counters sampled during runs, no per-stall correlation with burst timing). This project has retracted an unmeasured mechanism claim before — an earlier internal attribution of Tessera's own tail to probe-timeout backoff was falsified by counting probe firings — so the distinction is drawn deliberately. Alternatives the constraints do not rule out include SACK-reneging pathologies, qdisc/backlog interactions with the open-loop harness, and kernel buffer behaviour under 400 s of accumulated backlog. Section 6, threat 2 carries this.

**The inferred mechanism.** At 4.8 % Gilbert–Elliott loss in ~5-packet bursts, the retransmission of a lost packet has a materially elevated chance of dying in the same or an adjacent burst. An ARQ transport then has no arrival to trigger fast retransmit / RACK reordering logic on *that* data; the only remaining signal is the retransmission timer, and RTO backoff doubles per attempt. Two or three consecutive losses of the same segment produce a multi-second stall by arithmetic, not by defect. This is the shape visible in the well-provisioned ARQ rows — TLS and msquic: medians at or near the link floor, p99/p999 in seconds — and a worst-case TLS tail of 900 s where the sender could not sustain 50 msg/s under backpressure at all. (SCTP's and kwik's medians are *not* at the floor, for reasons given below and in Section 3.1 respectively.)

**Alibi 1 — "it's the congestion controller." Constrained, not eliminated.** kwik's lte rows are exactly this, and are labelled so throughout. msquic exists in the experiment to answer it: its loss *recovery* (RACK-style + PTO) is shared between its congestion controllers, and **under both CCs the p999 remains seconds-scale** — 3.0–6.6 s under CUBIC and 4.3–6.4 s under BBR, across 3 completing runs each. That is enough to say the congestion controller cannot account for the category: if the tail were window starvation, BBR — which does not read loss as congestion — should have moved it out of the seconds range, and it did not. It is *not* enough to say the two CCs are indistinguishable: 3 runs per arm, closed-loop, on one box, with one failed rep per CC excluded, supports no paired or ranked comparison between them — this project's own record shows ARQ tail spreads of 49.5× across runs of identical code, so overlapping 3-run ranges carry no finer verdict. We state Alibi 1 to the same standard as Alibi 2: the CC is constrained out as the category's cause; a within-category CC comparison would need many more runs. Production engineering is worth a real 4× (kernel-class ~20 s down to ~5 s), but a seconds-scale tail remains under both CCs.

**Alibi 2 — "it's head-of-line blocking." Suggestively constrained, not eliminated.** SCTP ran unordered: per-message reliability, no cross-message ordering, no stream to block. It still delivered fully in only 1 of 13 runs with p99 ≈ 11 s — *suggesting* that HOL is not required for the spiral. But this arm must carry the same scrutiny the paper applies to kwik: it landed on the campaign's final day, and the measurement log records no provisioning work for it — no socket-buffer sizing, SCTP RTO parameter review, or clean-link control run appears anywhere in the log. Its 291 ms median — ~3× the floor, where TLS and msquic sit at it — is unexplained and at least as consistent with an under-provisioned or defective arm (buffers, kernel `RTO_MIN`, JDK SCTP channel quirks) as with the timer mechanism. The project's own comparator lesson (kwik delivered 0/1500 until its buffers were raised: "a comparator only compares fairly when it is provisioned") was, so far as the log shows, never applied here. TLS alone cannot carry the HOL elimination, since TLS has HOL. We therefore state Alibi 2 as: the SCTP result is consistent with the timer account and inconsistent with HOL being *necessary*, but a properly provisioned SCTP arm is required before "eliminated" is the right word. Section 6, threat 3.

**Why FEC lacks *this* floor — at this rate.** Tessera's repair symbols are linear combinations over a sliding window; recovering a burst of b lost sources needs any b independent equations covering the window, not the survival of b specific retransmissions. There is no distinguished packet to lose twice. The residual ARQ path exists, and the FEC arm's tail physics — recovery ≈ burst-length × inter-message gap + RTT — was measured at 150–300 ms on the *in-process* NetemSim version of this profile (the kernel campaign's 311 ms p999 is consistent with that account, but the two numbers come from different instruments: the project's own sim-vs-kernel validation shows the sim reading the lte transport-level tail ~2× high, sim ~264 ms vs tc ~133 ms, with a loss-model residual still open — the sim's GE chain loses 6.35 % against the kernel's ~4.7 % at identical parameters). The kernel-measured p999 of ~311 ms is ~2.5× its 123 ms median; the source log states this ratio as "~3.4× its median," which is arithmetically inconsistent with its own table (311/123 ≈ 2.5; 3.4× instead matches the ~90 ms nominal link RTT, 311/90 ≈ 3.5) — we report the table's arithmetic and flag the log's discrepancy rather than silently reassigning its denominator. The FEC floor is real but it is RTT-and-rate-scale here, not timer-scale — and "rate-scale" cuts both ways: at 10 msg/s on the in-process lte profile the same transport's recorded p99 is **2.7 s** against 387 ms at 50 msg/s (`bench amp`, in-process NetemSim; open, unexplained item in the project's TODO). That figure cannot be translated to the kernel instrument by any correction factor: the sim's tail error is regime-dependent and non-multiplicative by the log's own cross-check — in the repair-clock A/B the sim reported a 4.1× effect where kernel netem measured 1.22×, the log's stated conclusion being that the sim's error interacts with the mechanism under test and cannot be divided out — and its known ~2× lte validation was measured at 2000 msg/s, a different regime; no kernel-netem 10 msg/s run exists. We therefore present the 2.7 s strictly as an in-process boundary signal. As such it is a category-scale signal that the FEC arm's tail is rate-dependent — which is why the claim in this paper is stated at 50 msg/s and not as a rate-free law — while the exact figure awaits a kernel-instrument measurement. Notably, the project's log falsified an earlier internal claim that Tessera's tail was probe-timeout backoff: measurement showed the PTO fires ~3 times per 2,000 messages and is structurally unreachable while the application keeps sending; the tail is equation accumulation. The distinction matters because it means the FEC transport's tail scales with message rate and burst statistics, while (under the inferred account) the ARQ transports' tails scale with timer backoff — different physics, hence the category gap at this operating point.

## 5 Methodology findings

### 5.1 Run length: the effective n is runs, not messages

The same TLS arm, same box, same qdisc, same rate: p99 ≈ 610 ms at n = 1,500 (30 pairs multi-box: median 609.4 ms); **p99 ≈ 19.2 s at n = 20,000**, with 5 of 20 runs failing full delivery and one run's tail at 900 s. This is not a contradiction but sampling: a 30-second run rarely lives long enough to contain a multi-second recovery stall; a 6.7-minute run contains one almost surely. The early SCTP rows show the same shape. Short runs were flattering the ARQ transports — this project's recurring failure mode (tail claims from mean-sized samples, several retracted in the log) caught pointing the *other* way for once. Corollaries: (a) report per-run tail statistics and cross-run ranges, never pooled-message percentiles; (b) a comparison's run length must be sized to the rarest event either arm can produce, which for ARQ under bursty loss is minutes, not seconds; (c) when a timeout guard can kill an invocation, arm order decides who starves — order by importance.

### 5.2 The GSO/qdisc artifact: a validity threat to every batching-sender netem benchmark

msquic's initial runs under the qdisc showed 1–16 RPS, 10 s tails, and entire 300-second runs completing **zero** requests — numbers that would have slandered a production transport. The cause is not msquic: it batches sends into UDP GSO superpackets, and **a qdisc drops a superpacket as one unit** — one netem loss event at the stated per-packet probability kills ~50 wire-packets, and the rate cap serializes 60 KB monsters. The stated loss process is simply not the applied loss process. `ethtool -K lo tx-udp-segmentation off` does **not** fix it (msquic probes GSO with a live send that still succeeds; the kernel software-segments after the qdisc either way); the sender itself must stop requesting segmentation (here: patching the datapath to honour `MSQUIC_NO_GSO`). Same 30 s smoke test, GSO on vs off: **p999 1.7 s → 505 ms — a 3.4× pure artifact**, and the smoke test is the *mild* form: the pre-fix 300 s campaign runs were far worse (zero completions, 10 s tails). The standing rule this yields: *any* netem measurement of a GSO-batching sender is invalid until segmentation is verifiably disabled at the sender — a zero-completion sanity run is the cheap tell. This applies to Tessera's own batched native datapath in future netem-on-Linux runs; the Java arms in this campaign all send per-datagram and are unaffected.

## 6 Threats to validity

Stated at full strength, because the FEC arm is the authors' own transport.

1. **One box per powered campaign.** The n = 20,000 ladder ran on five nodes for the Java arms but msquic's numbers are from one box (`ewr`); the 11-pair TLS result is one box. The multi-box lte result is box-independent (6/6 on every node), but the wifi-busy result is not (one node split 3/3) — heterogeneity a one-box experiment cannot see, demonstrated inside this very dataset.
2. **The mechanism is inferred, not traced.** No packet captures, kernel retransmit/RTO counters (`ss -ti`), or per-stall burst-timing correlations were collected for any ARQ arm. The lost-retransmit → RTO-backoff account is the explanation the alibi constraints leave standing and the arithmetic supports; it is not a measurement, and this project has previously had to retract exactly this class of unmeasured mechanism claim (the PTO misattribution for Tessera's own tail). Alternatives not excluded: SACK reneging, qdisc/backlog interaction with the open-loop harness, kernel buffer exhaustion under hundreds of seconds of backlog. Instrumented stall traces are the obvious next experiment.
3. **The SCTP arm is unverified as a comparator.** The log records no provisioning, tuning, or clean-link control-run work for it before it entered the campaign (only that the arm landed on the final day with powered runs in flight), and its 291 ms median — ~3× the floor where every well-provisioned arm sits at it — is unexplained. The same project once mis-measured kwik at 0/1500 delivery for want of a buffer setting. The HOL elimination is accordingly stated as suggestive, not established (Section 4), and the category placement of "kernel ARQ, no HOL" is provisional on a provisioned rerun.
4. **Echo doubles the qdisc.** Loopback puts one qdisc on both directions: every message crosses the impairment twice, acks and data share the rate cap, and the effective loss per round trip is roughly double the per-leg figure. All arms suffer it equally, but it is not any real link's topology.
5. **Message-shaped traffic favours the FEC design.** 1200 B independent messages at a fixed rate is Tessera's home turf. TCP and QUIC are stream transports being used message-wise; a bulk-stream throughput comparison would favour theirs and was not run here. Relatedly, Tessera's own bulk behaviour has an open unexplained item: `transcont` bulk sits at ~4 MB/s on a nominal 1 Gbit link with loss, cwnd, credit and flow control all shown non-binding — nobody can yet say why. The claim in this paper is a tail-latency claim about message workloads, nothing more.
6. **Closed-loop msquic vs open-loop everything else, from a separate uncontrolled pass** (Section 2.3). The loop asymmetry flatters msquic; it also means msquic's per-message latencies are not sampled from an identical offered-load process. In addition, the msquic measurements come from a separate later pass with **no interleaved control arm** — no concurrent raw-UDP or Tessera run beside them — so host and qdisc drift between the passes is unbounded except by the earlier pass's udp calibration rows; the interleaving guarantee of Section 2.1 covers the `vs` campaigns only. And at 3 completing runs per CC (one failed rep per CC excluded — the reported msquic tails therefore omit its worst runs entirely), the msquic rows support range statements, not medians or within-category comparisons. The category ordering is robust to all of this; the exact msquic magnitudes are not comparable to the open-loop rows at percentage precision.
7. **300 s msquic runs vs 400 s for the other arms.** Different run lengths under-sample msquic's rare events relative to the Java arms — again an asymmetry in msquic's favour, again unable to move a category boundary but able to move its numbers.
8. **Censoring.** Percentiles are over delivered messages only (Section 2.1); the ARQ arms' undelivered messages (TLS worst run 13,753/20,000; SCTP worst 1,564/20,000) do not enter any statistic, so the reported ARQ p999s are lower bounds. Conservative for the conclusion, but a real analytic choice.
9. **kwik rows are congestion-controller collapse, not QUIC.** Labelled so throughout; the fair kwik comparison is wifi-busy only. The production-QUIC claim rests on msquic alone.
10. **Cross-pass rows are not pair-matched.** The final p999 table combines two passes; the number of shared-minute pairs is smaller than the row counts suggest. At these magnitudes (hundreds of ms vs tens of seconds) it cannot matter; at percentage-level differences it would.
11. **Tessera is an unaudited research prototype** with a v0 wire format, no interop evidence, no formal analysis beyond the Noise-IK provenance of its handshake, and a security assurance not comparable to TLS's regardless of any latency number. Its FEC overhead is also a real cost this paper does not charge against it: at low rates including the campaign's 50 msg/s, the measured overhead is **2.15–2.37× wire bytes per payload byte** (every message gets a trailing repair); at high rates it amortizes — the log's all-profile 2000 msg/s kernel-netem run records overheads of **1.14–1.33×** by profile (its lte re-baseline table spans 1.125–1.276). On a metered or narrow uplink the low-rate figure is the wrong trade (measured live: the overhead saturated a ~0.56 Mbit hotspot uplink that raw UDP fit).
12. **One profile and one rate at depth — and the boundary evidence is from a different instrument.** The p999 campaign is lte at 50 msg/s under kernel netem only. wifi-busy already shows the edge narrowing to 1.25× (and not universal) under light random loss; and at 10 msg/s the FEC arm's own recorded p99 is 2.7 s against 387 ms at 50 msg/s — but those figures come from `bench amp` on the *in-process* NetemSim lte profile, not from kernel netem. No kernel-netem 10 msg/s run exists, and the sim's error cannot be corrected for: its documented lte tail overstatement (sim ~264 ms vs tc ~133 ms) was measured at 2000 msg/s, a different regime, and the log's own repair-clock cross-check (sim 4.1× vs kernel 1.22× for the same A/B) concludes that the sim's error interacts with the mechanism under test and is not a scale factor that can be divided out. The 2.7 s is therefore strictly an in-process boundary signal: category-scale evidence that the FEC-side tail is rate-dependent, with the kernel-instrument figure unmeasured. It remains an open, unexplained item that bounds the FEC-side claim by rate, not only the ARQ-side claim by loss model. Profiles are named after technologies they are not (a lesson this project's log records at length — its simulator's tail was itself wrong by 2–3× until a 4-sigma clamp matching the kernel's tables was found). The category claim is specifically about *bursty* loss at this rate.
13. **Emulation, not radios.** Live 5G-hotspot sessions in the same log show non-stationarity that exceeds mechanism effects at feasible run counts; nothing here predicts a specific radio.

## 7 Related work

- **QUIC loss recovery.** [CITE: RFC 9002, QUIC loss detection and congestion control] specifies the PTO/RTO machinery whose backoff behaviour under repeated loss is the inferred mechanism here; [CITE: RFC 9000/9001, QUIC transport and TLS mapping]. Tessera borrows several QUIC wire mechanisms by specification (varints, truncated packet numbers, ACK ranges, header protection, key update, amplification limits, MAX_DATA-shape flow control — per its NOTICE provenance) while containing no code derived from any QUIC implementation and no BBR.
- **RACK.** [CITE: RACK-TLP, time-based loss detection for TCP] — the recovery style shared by the kernel TCP and msquic arms; this paper measures the regime where, on the inferred account, even time-based detection falls through to the timer because the retransmit itself is lost.
- **Receiver-driven scheduling.** Homa [CITE: Ousterhout et al., Homa: a receiver-driven low-latency transport] is the provenance of Tessera's credit-based congestion control, including grant re-issue on timeout — relevant here because a CC that does not read random loss as congestion is what keeps the FEC arm's median at the floor while kwik's collapses.
- **RLNC / sliding-window FEC.** [CITE: sliding-window random linear network coding literature] is the provenance of the recovery mechanism; [CITE: FEC-for-transport work, e.g. coding extensions proposed for QUIC] situates FEC-vs-ARQ trade-offs, to which this paper adds a deep-tail measurement under matched kernel impairment.
- **CUBIC / HyStart++.** [CITE: RFC 9438; RFC 9406] — the kernel arm's CC and Tessera's loss-based fallback, implemented from the documents.
- **LEDBAT.** [CITE: RFC 6817] — measured against Tessera elsewhere in the project's log (Tessera yields *harder* than the scavenger, inverting the pre-registered prediction); included as provenance for the coexistence posture, not used in this paper's ladder.
- **Handshake provenance.** Noise IK [CITE: Perrin, Noise Protocol Framework; Kobeissi & Bhargavan, Noise Explorer] and ML-KEM [CITE: FIPS 203]; orthogonal to the tail result but part of the measured system.

## 8 Conclusion

Under identical kernel-netem bursty-loss impairment, at run lengths that sample rare events, the p999 of message delivery on this profile at 50 msg/s separates by recovery mechanism into categories: ~0.31 s for FEC-based recovery, ~3–6.6 s for production timer-driven ARQ (seconds-scale under either congestion controller, though 3 runs per CC support no finer comparison between them), and ~17–22 s for kernel ARQ (with the no-HOL row provisional on a provisioned SCTP rerun) — a ~9.6–21× gap against ARQ's best case, widening to ~54–70× against the kernel arms. The explanation left standing — inferred from the alibi constraints, not traced — is the lost retransmit: ARQ's last resort is a backoff timer, and timers cost seconds; FEC's last resort is another equation, and equations cost a message gap. Production engineering moves an ARQ transport within its category — a real 4× — but did not move it out under either congestion controller. The result is bounded exactly as measured: one bursty-loss profile at one rate (the FEC arm's own p99 reaches 2.7 s at a 5× lower rate, an in-process measurement with no kernel-instrument counterpart), message workloads, emulation, censored percentiles on the incomplete arms, and a prototype on the FEC side whose 2.15–2.37× low-rate overhead and unaudited assurance are not free. The methodology results travel further than the transport does: tail claims need run-sized samples of run-sized events, and a netem benchmark of a batching sender is measuring a different network than it names.
